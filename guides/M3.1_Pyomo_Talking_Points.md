## 1 · Why a solver, and why only now

**Excel was the old way, and it didn't scale.** You highlighted cells, rows and columns in a spreadsheet, and that was really limiting. Being able to say `read_csv` and hand the table straight to the model is what lets you build something real.

**The order was deliberate.** Brute force, graphical, Simplex — all by hand — *then* the solver. Now that you have a magic box that solves optimization problems and you understand the theory of how the problems get crafted, **it's less important to investigate how the solver works and more important to set the problem up with the data and get the answer.** You can go back to the algorithm later.

**It solved in 0.05 seconds.** That's the point: the algorithm stopped being the bottleneck, so **your modelling judgment is now the scarce thing.**

## 2 · The five things

Import → **`ConcreteModel()`** → **`Var`** → **`Objective`** → **`Constraint`**. Then solve and inspect.

**`ConcreteModel()` is a blank canvas.** Everything after that is an *attribute you append to it* — `model.c`, `model.profit`, `model.fabrication`. It looks like accessing a pandas column; it's really building an object up piece by piece.

**`domain=NonNegativeReals` is the most important argument you'll type.** It's the non-negativity constraint you kept forgetting to write by hand in Module 2, expressed once.

**Naming is a real choice.** `Constraint1, Constraint2, Constraint3` is the engineering approach and it's fine. `model.fabrication` is better, and **it shows you're thinking about the problem one constraint at a time.**

**You declared 10 things** — three variables, one objective, six constraints. Most people never count, and counting is what makes `pprint()` useful: it's an **audit**, and it's literally the file that goes to the solver.

**The parentheses trap:** `model.profit` prints garbage. **`model.profit()` prints your answer.**

## 3 · The three shapes of an LP

| Shape | You are… | Direction | Constraints |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Allocation** | handing out a limited resource among competing uses | maximize | all $\le$ |
| **Covering** | meeting every minimum standard as cheaply as possible | minimize | all $\ge$ |
| **Blending** | hitting a weighted-average specification | either | needs algebra first |

**Once you can classify a problem into one of these three, most of the modelling decisions are already made.** Allocation is the most common thing you'll solve in business.

## 4 · Binding constraints and shadow prices

**Binding means the left-hand side equals the right-hand side** — you used every last unit. **Binding constraints are the bottlenecks. They're where the friction is. They're the thing jamming up your business and preventing you from making more money.**

**A shadow price is the profit from one additional unit of a binding resource.** It's only meaningful for binding constraints — a resource you already aren't using fully is worth nothing more.

**The business test is a comparison, not a number.** If an extra fabrication hour yields \$4 of profit and costs \$1 to run the machine, **any boss takes that deal.** The shadow price only means something against the real cost.

**And it decays.** As a resource becomes abundant, each extra unit is worth less, until it's worth nothing. **Diamonds versus sand** — diamonds are rare so they're valuable; sand is everywhere. Push fabrication hours far enough and they turn from diamonds into sand, usually because **the bottleneck has moved somewhere else in the factory.**

## 5 · The transparent way to do sensitivity analysis

**Put the model in a for loop.** Vary the right-hand side of the binding constraint across a range, re-solve every time, store the results. That's the whole technique — **you haven't done anything mathematically heavy; you've just stuck a model inside a loop.**

**Buffer both sides** of your current value so you can see the behaviour before *and* after where you actually sit.

**Total profit is a misleading plot** — of course profit rises when you add capacity. **The informative quantity is the difference**: `.diff()` on profit divided by your step size gives **profit per additional hour**, which *is* the shadow price, measured rather than asserted.

**Watch the product mix, not just the money.** As capacity grows the recipe changes — tables first, then desks, then desks hit their demand ceiling and **chairs finally become worth building.** That non-linear response is the model telling you where its own logic switches.

**There are other ways to get shadow prices.** This one is transparent, and that's why we use it.

## 6 · Optimal is still not the same as sensible

**The trail mix proves it again.** The optimizer returns half a pound of seeds, a third of raisins and 1.3 pounds of coconut flakes for **\$7.53**. Every nutritional constraint is satisfied. **Nobody would eat it.**

**The fix is in the model, not the math.** Add a floor of a little of everything and you get something you could sell for **\$8.33** — and that 80-cent difference is *the price of the promise you're making to your customer.*

**Constraints or bounds — your call.** Those minimums could have been five new constraints or just tighter `bounds`. Same model, different readability. *I kind of like seeing the constraints.*

## 7 · Why blending needs algebra

**A weighted average is a fraction, and solvers dislike fractions.** So you clear it: multiply both sides by the denominator, collect terms, and you get something like $-3B - 18C + 7P \ge 0$.

**That doesn't look interpretable at first blush** — and it doesn't have to be. **It's the same constraint, just scaled.** Check it by setting every variable to 1 and watching the weighted average collapse into the simple average.

**Then read the answer back as a story.** Aroma is binding. Brazilian beans are cheap and aromatic, so you use all of them. Peruvian gets you the rest of the way to 78 but costs more, so you're careful. Colombian is weak on aroma but strong on strength — **so you sprinkle a couple in to reach 16 and no further.** You can work all of that out from the constraints and the decision variables alone.
