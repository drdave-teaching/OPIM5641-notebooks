## Notebook 1 · `5_Simplex/0_BigIdeas_Simplex_vs_Graphical.ipynb` — videos 16–20

**Video 16 — Welcome video to Simplex algorithm** *(3:56)*
The motivating example, and the reason everything else exists. Takes stock of the two methods already in hand: brute force was **transparent but horrible**, the graphical method was **fantastic but two-dimensional**. Then the definition that carries the module — Simplex is an **iterative, algebraic technique**, the *algebraic enhancement of the geometric graphical method* — and the promise that pays for it: three variables, five, **forty**.

**Video 17 — The geometry of Simplex** *(6:13)*
The corner walk, before any algebra. Defines **adjacency** properly (two corner points are adjacent if they **share a constraint boundary**) and states the **optimality test** in full. Then walks Wyndor by eye: greed picks the direction (\$3,000 across $x_1$ vs. \$5,000 up $x_2$ — *you're going to be greedy*), and the crucial distinction lands — $(0,9)$ is a corner point but **not a corner point *feasible* solution**, which is exactly the job the minimum ratio test will do. Ends at $(2,6)$, and flags what's tested and what isn't.

**Video 18 — Augmented form, basic vs. nonbasic variables** *(8:48)*
Five key concepts, then the vocabulary. **Slack = surplus = the unused quantity** — and the environmental-engineering story that makes the origin feel earned: at a contaminated site the first candidate solution is genuinely *do nothing*, just like manufacturing nothing is a bad-but-feasible plan. Then augmented form (one slack per constraint), augmented solutions, basic solutions, **degrees of freedom**, and the line the rest of the course rests on: **non-basic variables are ZERO, basic variables are NON-zero.** *Notice how it's kind of backwards — write it on a piece of paper.*

**Video 19 — The minimum ratio test** *(8:00)*
The properties of a basic solution, then **the steps to commit to memory**: initialize at the origin → optimality test → where to go → where to stop → solve → test again. Initializes Wyndor at $(0,0,4,12,18)$ making zero profit, picks $x_2$ as the **entering basic variable**, and then does the thing the video is named for: since you can't *see* five-dimensional space, you need math to tell you where you smack into the first constraint. $x_2 \le 6$ beats $x_2 \le 9$, so you stop at $(0,6)$. Sets up Gauss-Jordan as the cleanup.

**Video 20 — Gauss-Jordan Elimination and wrapping up long-hand math** *(7:47)*
Why the row operations are legal (**multiply a line by a constant, it's the same line**), why the bottom row carries minus signs, and the sign convention that does all the work: **a negative number in the objective row means there's still a rate of improvement.** Runs iteration 2 to $(2,6)$, $Z = 36$, no negatives left, done. Closes on the **aha moment** — 10 million → five → three — and states plainly that this longhand video is **not** assessed, but the next one is.

## Notebook 2 · `5_Simplex/1_General Simplex Maximization Steps.ipynb` — video 21

**Video 21 — Your mental map: general Simplex steps for a 2D maximization problem** *(4:45)*
*"Whoa — did your hair get blown back by that example?"* The cheat sheet, compressed: augmented form → initial tableau → biggest negative in the bottom row → minimum ratio test → pivot element → make it a 1 → Gauss-Jordan with **"the opposite of whatever's sitting there, times your new special row"** → read off → optimality test. Also sets expectations for assessments: in the real world you iterate to optimality, but on many of Dave's assessments you pivot **once** to prove the mechanics.

## Notebook 3 · `5_Simplex/Wyndor_Glass_Simplex_Method.ipynb` — video 22

**Video 22 — Simplex for Wyndor end-to-end** *(10:22)*
**This is how you'll be evaluated.** Word problem → augmented form (*"the first 10 points"*) → initial tableau (*"another 10 points"*) → two pivots → read off. Every ratio and row operation is spoken. Lands $x_1 = 2$, $x_2 = 6$, $Z = 36$, and then introduces **binding vs. non-binding constraints** — slack left over means wiggle room; slack driven to zero means that constraint is genuinely squeezing you, which is the seed of sensitivity analysis in Module 3. Closes the Rosetta Stone: *now I've shown you how to solve a problem three ways.*
