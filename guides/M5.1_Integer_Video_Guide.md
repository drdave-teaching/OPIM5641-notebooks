# M5.1 Video Guide — Integer Programming

**OPIM 5641 - Business Decision Modeling · Dr. Dave Wanik · University of Connecticut**

Modules 3 and 4 optimized *quantities* — how much to make, blend, or invest. This module changes the question: some decisions are whole numbers, and some are pure **yes/no**. The payoff is the **linking constraint**, one line of algebra that couples a yes/no to a how-much — and suddenly the solver can answer *"should we even be in this business?"*

## The arc in one line

**Force whole numbers and pay a dollar for it → make decisions binary and pick a project portfolio → write the committee's rules as one-line constraints → link yes/no to how-much, and let the model fire a sales team.**

## Notebook 1 · `8_Integer/Integer_Furniture.ipynb`

**Video 1 — Introduction to integer programming** *(5:26)*
The furniture problem with one nudge — fabrication hours from 1,850 to 1,800 — so the LP answer goes fractional: **$8,200 with 266.67 desks**. Dave walks both honest responses: lop off the decimal (a model is "an abstraction of some business problem"; 1,776.2 trucks ≈ 1,776), or make wholeness part of the model with the one-word change `domain=NonNegativeIntegers`. The integer answer: **$8,199 — one chair, 266 desks, 100 tables**. Two framings to keep: **constraints are red tape** (his Wall Street riff — rules cap the profit), and the geometric view that an integer requirement *shrinks the feasible region*, so the objective can only hold or fall.

## Notebook 2 · `8_Integer/Integer_Project Selection.ipynb`

**Video 2 — Project Selection Introduction (Binary Variables)** *(7:11)*
Capital budgeting pitched at his grant-specialist students: **$160M, five projects** (IT system, licensed tech, recycling facility, machining center, receiving move), each with an NPV and a cost — and the honest preamble that capital projects never return what they cost. Then the deliberate mistake: declare the y's `NonNegativeIntegers`, and the solver happily "does project four five times" for a fake **$40M**. The diagnosis — *the model will always run; the domain was wrong* — and the fix, `Binary`, landing at **$34M with the budget spent to the dollar**. Sets the block's standing rule: watch the objective fall as rules stack. *(He signs off saying "project one and project four" — the actual slate is P1/P3/P4, and video 3 opens with the correct recap.)*

**Video 3 — Basic project selection constraints** *(5:25)*
The world's-smallest-pen scoreboard starts. Three policies, each one line: **at least m** (projects 2 and 5 are international, management wants one: y2+y5 ≥ 1 → **$32M, projects 1/4/5, only $144M spent**), **at most n** (oversight capacity of two: sum ≤ 2 → **$31M, projects 2/5**), **exactly k** (swap ≤ for =). Every rule gets a portfolio analogy — "at least three stocks from this sector," "exactly two from each sector."

**Video 4 — Advanced project selection examples** *(4:47)*
The two grown-up relationships. **Mutual exclusivity**: y4+y5 ≤ 1 — his NVIDIA/AMD line: pick one, the other, or neither, never both. **Contingency**: y3 − y5 ≥ 0 — no company-wide Gmail unless you've signed with Google first; he walks the truth table case by case and flags that **the order of the subtraction is the whole constraint**. The master model with everything on lands at **$30M, projects 3 and 5**, closing the slide $40M → $34M → $32M → $31M → $30M.

## Notebook 3 · `8_Integer/Fixed_VariableCosts_Mayhugh.ipynb`

**Video 5 — Introduction to linking constraints / Mayhugh manufacturing** *(4:49)*
"One of my favorite examples in the series." The textbook job shop retold as a **pharmaceutical company**: three drugs (heart, cancer, arthritis), three departments, and — the new ingredient — a **dedicated sales team per product line** ($60K / $200K / $100K). Scenario 1 is the analyst's shortcut: assume you always pay all three teams, subtract $360K as a constant, and solve the familiar product mix → **$410 at 400/100/50**. The setup for everything after: this model was never allowed to ask whether a product line should exist.

**Video 6 — Linking constraints for product maximums (fire the sales team)** *(5:44)*
The module's centerpiece. The demand ceiling becomes **x ≤ M·y** — rearranged to x − M·y ≤ 0 "to play nicely with the solvers" — and each sales team's cost moves into the objective attached to its own y. Run it and the solver **drops product line 3 entirely**: you make roughly **20% more by *not* doing something**. Ends on the consultant's conscience, worth quoting whole: *"those are people in the sales department, and your model said, let them go."*

**Video 7 — Linking constraints for product minimums** *(3:57)*
The threshold constraint **x − m·y ≥ 0**, taught through his portfolio rule — "if you're going to invest in this stock, invest at least 2%" — with the arithmetic checked out loud (10% in? 10−2 ≥ 0 ✓. 1% in? 1−2 < 0 ✗). Applied to Mayhugh: if you keep product 2's sales force, make **at least 250,000 units**. Production jumps 160 → 250, profit steps down to **$490**, F3 stays gone. Closes the module on the modeling moral: constant-fixed-cost vs. linked-fixed-cost are *both valid models* — your assumptions decide what the solver is allowed to discover. *(One slip: he says "instead of making $510" — scenario 2 was $508.)*
