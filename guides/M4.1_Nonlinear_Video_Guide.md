## Notebook 1 · `7_Nonlinear/Polynomial.ipynb`

**Video 1 — Polynomial and intro to nonlinear optimization** *(7:51)*
Why nonlinear exists at all: powers, roots, exponents, stepwise functions — **a linear solver won't take any of them** — plus the point that *one* nonlinear curve often beats a dozen linear pieces stitched together. Then the demonstration that earns the whole module. Price an iPhone to maximize units sold; initialize at **0** and the solver reports **optimal: 594 units at p = 1.95**. Initialize at **9** and it reports **optimal: 900 units at p = 8.36**. *"You bring your code over and I bring my code over, and we're trying to figure out who got the right answer."*

The answer is **the hill climber in the fog** — you're halfway up a mountain, fog rolls in, and all you can do is take a step and ask *am I going up or down?* You climb until you can't, and announce a summit. **It just isn't necessarily the highest one.** Closes on bounding: constrain $p$ to $[1,7]$, start at 5, and it stops dead at **p = 7, objective 684** — *"if you're getting an answer sitting right at a boundary, I would pause and reevaluate."*

## Notebook 2 · `7_Nonlinear/Pharmacy - Complete_guided.ipynb`

**Video 2 — Setting up our first regression** *(8:18)*
**Casting regression as an optimization problem** — Dave's favourite way in, and the bridge to every stats and ML class students have taken. CVS pharmacies: hours open vs. revenue, 10 stores. `scipy.linregress` first for a reference answer (**intercept ≈ \$4,435, slope ≈ \$47 per extra hour open**), then the rebuild in Pyomo.

**The twist is `model.y`** — an empty placeholder decision variable, one slot per store. *"Up until now you haven't seen where the linear form of the model lives."* It lives in the **constraints**: one per data point, each forcing a prediction to equal $a + bx_i$. **10 stores → 10 predictions → 10 constraints.** *"I know it's a little bit crazy."*

**Video 3 — Different flavors of objective function** *(7:48)*
Reading the machinery back off the pretty print, then the payoff: **Pyomo and scipy agree exactly**, so you know it works. Then the playing starts. Swap squared error for **absolute error** — and it **errors out**, because absolute value has a V with a point and *"functions with a point aren't easily differentiated."* Apply the fix, get a **genuinely different line.** Then bend the model itself: `x ** model3.b` with the exponent as a **free parameter**, lower error, better-looking shape. *"If I were your boss and you showed me these three candidate models, I like the blue one — it's getting the spirit of it rather than fitting the outlier."*

**Video 4 — Wrapping up casting regression as an optimization model** *(3:37)*
Power model with absolute error — better number, **but flagged honestly: it's on different units, so you can't compare it directly.** Then the deliberately silly one: seven hard-coded root terms, a curve that snakes through every point. *"It's like when you go into Excel and you just keep cranking the polynomial order up, up, up. This is clearly overfitting."* Lands the two levers — **the form of the model** and **the objective function** — and the judgment call: *"you'd never turn that model in to your boss."*

## Notebook 3 · `7_Nonlinear/Location Problem_Simple_guided.ipynb`

**Video 5 — Intro to simple warehouse location problem** *(6:26)*
Why it's nonlinear and can't be otherwise: **distance is a square root.** A paper company with 10 big-box stores needs one distribution center. And then the recognition that makes the module cohere — **it's the pharmacy problem again.** A placeholder (`model.Dist`, one slot per store), one constraint per store forcing it to equal the distance formula, then sum and minimize. *"Very sneaky, just like the linear form of the model."*

## Notebook 4 · `7_Nonlinear/Location Problem_Advanced_guided.ipynb`

**Video 6 — Advanced warehouse location problem** *(6:39)*
Solves the simple version first — **sum of distances 670, DC at (114, 35)** — then asks the real question: *what if one store took 90% of the deliveries?* Add a column `n`, **multiply each distance by its store's trip count inside the constraint**, and nothing else changes. Store 2 gets spiked to **100 trips** — *"this is like Jupiter, right?"* — and the center of mass visibly drags toward it. Closes by naming the pattern out loud one final time (placeholder + one constraint per row + sum) and pointing at California Housing, scraped CVS locations, and Amazon distribution centers as places to take it.
