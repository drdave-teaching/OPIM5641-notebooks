# M5.1 Talking Points — Integer Programming

**OPIM 5641 - Business Decision Modeling · Dr. Dave Wanik · University of Connecticut**

The theory you should be able to explain out loud — to a classmate, to an interviewer, or to a boss who wants to know why the model says to fire the sales team.

---

## 1. Constraints are red tape

**The claim:** adding a rule can never improve the objective. Not usually — never.

**Why:** every constraint shrinks the feasible region. Fewer candidate solutions means the best survivor is the same or worse than before. Dave's framing: **if Wall Street had no rules, they'd make as much money as they want** — rules exist precisely to cap the objective.

**The modelling consequence:** you can *price* any policy by the drop it causes. The international-project rule cost $2M (34→32). The oversight cap cost another $1M. Mayhugh's minimum-batch policy cost $18K (508→490). When a stakeholder asks "what does this rule cost us?", the model answers in dollars.

---

## 2. An integer requirement is a constraint in disguise

**The claim:** switching `NonNegativeReals` → `NonNegativeIntegers` is not a formatting choice; it's a constraint, and you pay for it.

**Why:** geometrically, the feasible region contracts — only the whole-number points survive. Furniture: $8,200 → $8,199, and the answer doesn't *round*, it *moves* (a chair appears out of nowhere). Rounding 266.67 desks to 266 is not guaranteed optimal, or even feasible.

**When to skip it:** when the leftover is meaningless at scale — 1,776.2 trucks is 1,776. A model is an abstraction of a business problem; sometimes the honest move is the shrug.

---

## 3. A binary variable is a decision, not a count

**The claim:** y ∈ {0,1} models "do it or don't" — and using the wrong domain produces confident nonsense.

**Why:** with `NonNegativeIntegers`, the project-selection model "did project four five times" — $40M of value from installing the same machining center five times. The model ran fine. The answer was garbage. **"The model will always run — it's up to you to give it the right domain."**

**The general lesson:** solvers never validate your modeling choices. Every domain, bound, and constraint is a claim about reality, and wrong claims produce fluent, plausible, wrong answers.

---

## 4. Business logic is one-line algebra on binaries

**The claim:** almost any committee sentence becomes a single constraint on 0/1 variables.

| The sentence | The line |
|---|---|
| "at least one international project" | $y_2 + y_5 \ge 1$ |
| "we can only oversee two" | $\sum y_j \le 2$ |
| "exactly two flagships" | $\sum y_j = 2$ |
| "NVIDIA or AMD, not both" | $y_4 + y_5 \le 1$ |
| "no Gmail rollout without signing Google" | $y_3 - y_5 \ge 0$ |

**The subtle one is contingency:** $y_3 - y_5 \ge 0$ bans exactly one row of the truth table — (y3=0, y5=1). The *order* of the subtraction encodes which project depends on which; flip it and you've written a different business rule.

---

## 5. The linking constraint: x ≤ M·y

**The claim:** this one line couples "how much" (x, continuous) to "do we" (y, binary) — and it's the single most reusable trick in this module.

**Why it works:** if y = 0, the right side is 0 and x is forced to 0 — no production, and the objective never pays the fixed cost. If y = 1, the fixed cost is paid and x gets its full ceiling back. **M is just an upper bound on x** — in Mayhugh, the demand ceiling was already sitting there, doing double duty.

**Why it matters:** fixed costs are everywhere — sales teams, factory startups, setup fees, software licenses. Without linking, you subtract them as a constant and the model can't question them. With linking, "should this product line exist?" becomes a variable the solver sets.

---

## 6. The model that fired the sales team

**The claim:** Mayhugh's scenario 2 is the module's proof that reframing beats re-solving.

**Why:** same factory, same hours, same demands. Scenario 1 (fixed costs as a constant) says $410 and keeps all three lines. Scenario 2 (linked fixed costs) says **drop F3 entirely and make $508** — about 20% more profit by *not doing something*. The LP couldn't see it because the question wasn't representable.

**The consultant's coda, verbatim:** *"you build these models, and it's like — those are people in the sales department, and your model said, let them go."* Optimization outputs are recommendations about people. Say that out loud before you present one.

---

## 7. Thresholds: the floor that creates all-in or all-out

**The claim:** $x - m\,y \ge 0$ makes participation lumpy on purpose.

**Why:** paired with the ceiling $x - M\,y \le 0$, y=1 traps x in $[m, M]$ and y=0 collapses it to exactly zero. Portfolio version: "if you're in a stock at all, be in for at least 2%" — no breadcrumbs. Mayhugh version: keep F2's sales force only if you make at least 250K units → production jumps 160 → 250 and profit steps down $508 → $490.

**The closing moral:** constant-fixed-cost and linked-fixed-cost are both legitimate models of the same company. Modeling assumptions aren't overhead — they decide which truths the solver is *allowed to discover*.
