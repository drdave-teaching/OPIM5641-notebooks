## 1. Why you cannot just maximize return

**The claim:** "Buy the stock with the highest average monthly return" is arithmetically correct and professionally negligent.

**Why:** the average hides the ride. Computer averages ~2% a month, but gets there through months of +20% and months of -20%. A client near retirement does not experience an average - they experience the sequence. **Up 20%, down 50%, up 70%, down 3% is "too wild of a ride"** even if it ends in a good number.

**The modelling consequence:** you cannot maximize return and minimize risk in the same objective function - **you have to choose one.** So we maximize return and push risk into a *constraint*, then sweep that constraint across a range of risk buckets.

---

## 2. Diversification is not "own more things"

**The claim:** holding five different stocks does not make you diversified.

**Why:** if two of them are NVIDIA and AMD, they move as one. You own five tickers and roughly four independent bets. **Diversification is about co-movement, not count.**

**The Caribbean version** - the cleanest way to say it:
> In the rainy season tourists buy umbrellas. In the sunny season they buy ice cream. Each company alone makes money half the year and loses it the other half. Hold both and you earn the same return with far less volatility, because when one falls the other rises.

That is modern portfolio theory. Everything after it is bookkeeping.

## 3. Covariance, and what its parts mean

$$\text{Cov}(x,y)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}{N-1}$$

**Read the numerator out loud:** each period, take how far x was from *its own* average, times how far y was from *its own* average. Both above, or both below, gives a positive product. One up while the other is down gives a negative one.

**Three readings:**
- **Positive** → they move together → bad for a portfolio, they crash together too
- **Negative** → they move opposite → this is the pair you want
- **Zero** → genuinely independent

**The dream:** *"if you could achieve a 7% return on a mix of stocks whose covariance is zero, you just make 7% all day long."* You will never quite get there - but you can push covariance around to build different risk buckets.

**Why the diagonal is always positive:** a stock always moves with itself. `(13-10)×(13-10) = 9`, and `(7-10)×(7-10) = -3 × -3 = +9`. Negatives square away. **`Cov(i,i)` is just the variance of stock i.**

---

## 4. Why the model eats covariance, not correlation

**This is the question every sharp student asks**, and it has a clean three-part answer.

Correlation is covariance divided by both standard deviations:

$$\rho_{ij} = \frac{\text{Cov}(i,j)}{\sigma_i \sigma_j}$$

So you *could* write portfolio variance with correlation - but only by carrying both standard deviations along and multiplying them back in. Covariance already has them baked in.

**1. Units.** Portfolio risk must come out in return², so its square root is comparable to the return you're maximizing. Correlation is unitless - a "risk" built from it has no financial meaning.

**2. The diagonal.** `Cov(i,i)` is each stock's own variance. `Corr(i,i)` is **1.0 for every stock, always**. Build on correlation and you have silently declared that a jumpy tech stock and a sleepy utility carry identical standalone risk - and the optimizer will happily load up on the riskiest name, because you deleted the evidence.

**3. Magnitude.** Two utilities correlated at 0.8 and two biotechs correlated at 0.8 are not the same bet. Correlation cannot tell them apart; covariance can.

**The line to remember:**
> **Correlation is for your eyes. Covariance is for the optimizer.**

Correlation is normalized so a human can compare any two pairs at a glance. Covariance keeps the scale - and the scale is what portfolio risk is made of. In the notebook, feeding the correlation matrix to the risk formula gives an answer **~204× too big** and in meaningless units.

## 5. What makes this problem nonlinear

**Return is linear:**
$$\text{return} = \sum_i w_i \, r_i$$
A plain weighted average. A linear solver eats this happily.

**Risk is quadratic:**
$$\sigma^2_p = \sum_i \sum_j w_i \, w_j \, \text{Cov}(i,j)$$
Every term multiplies **two decision variables together**. That product is the whole reason you need IPOPT instead of GLPK.

**And it is not incidental - it is the point.** The `w_i × w_j` term is exactly what encodes "these two are held *together*." Take the nonlinearity out and you take diversification out with it.

**Why the weighting matters practically:** summing the raw covariance matrix gives the same number every time, no matter what you hold. Weighting each of the 25 cells by `prop_i × prop_j` is what lets risk respond to the allocation - it makes the matrix *dynamic* across risk buckets.

## 6. Reading the frontier like an advisor

**The allocation plot:** at low risk tolerance you hold a little of everything; as tolerance rises the model concentrates, and at the top it puts everything into auto.

**The risk/reward plot:** **look for the top-left corner.**

The argument to make to a client is about *marginal* return, not total:
> At a risk of 0.004 you earn about 0.02. Double that risk and you gain roughly **10% more return** - while putting all your money into a single stock.

**You don't have to double your risk to get twice the return - and past a point, doubling your risk barely buys you anything.** That flattening is the entire case for not betting the farm.

**Concretely:** the even split carries risk 0.001397; 80% into Auto carries 0.004813. **3.4× the risk** for a marginal gain in return.

## 7. Honest caveats worth saying out loud

- **The risk limits are scale-dependent.** The 0.001 / 0.0015 / 0.002 ladder works for this toy table and will mean nothing on real market data. Students trip here every year.
- **Some solves come back infeasible** because of very small numbers, and we discard them. That's acceptable here - but know that you are doing it.
- **Past returns are the input.** Everything above assumes the covariance you measured is the covariance you'll get. It isn't. Say so to a client.
- **This notebook is deliberately unglamorous.** It's written for accessibility, not elegance - and making it more sophisticated is part of the work you'll do on it.
