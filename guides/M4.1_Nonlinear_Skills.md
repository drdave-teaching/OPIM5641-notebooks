## 🏔️ Why nonlinear, and what it costs you
*Notebook: `Polynomial.ipynb`*

- [ ] Name what makes a model nonlinear: **a power, a square root, an exponent, a stepwise function**
- [ ] Say why a **linear solver won't take it**, and why we switch to **IPOPT**
- [ ] Explain why **one nonlinear curve** can beat a dozen linear pieces stitched together
- [ ] Recite the three habits: **initialize** your decision variables, **bound** them where you can, and **use a linear model if you can get away with it** — they're more reliable

## 🌫️ Local vs. global optima
*Notebook: `Polynomial.ipynb`*

- [ ] Explain **the hill climber in the fog** to someone who's never seen an optimizer
- [ ] Demonstrate the problem: the *same* model reports **optimal at p = 1.95 (594 units)** from one starting value and **optimal at p = 8.36 (900 units)** from another
- [ ] Say the uncomfortable part out loud: **"optimal" from a nonlinear solver means "no better neighbour", not "best possible"**
- [ ] **Plot the objective function** before trusting any answer, so you can see how many hills there are
- [ ] Defend a solution by **trying a range of starting values** rather than one lucky one
- [ ] Recognize a **solution stuck at a boundary** (bounds $[1,7]$ → answer lands exactly on 7) and treat it as a signal to reevaluate

## 📈 Regression as an optimization problem
*Notebooks: `Pharmacy - Complete_guided.ipynb` · `General Framework_Regression in Pyomo.ipynb`*

- [ ] Explain why **fitting a line IS an optimization problem** — decision variables, an objective, constraints
- [ ] Write the model as $y = a + bx$ and identify **$a$ and $b$ as the decision variables**
- [ ] Define error **as a distance**, and say why squaring it **emphasizes outliers**

- [ ] Build the **placeholder decision variable** `model.y` — one slot per data point
- [ ] Write **one constraint per data point** forcing each prediction to equal the model form

- [ ] Confirm your Pyomo answer **matches scipy exactly** — that agreement is the check

## 🎛️ Choosing the model and the objective
*Notebook: `Pharmacy - Complete_guided.ipynb`*

- [ ] Swap **squared error** for **absolute error** and explain why the fit changes
- [ ] Say why `abs()` breaks the solver: **the V has a point, and points aren't easily differentiated**
- [ ] Apply the reformulation, and know it works **only because you're minimizing**
- [ ] Bend the model: `x ** model.b` with the **exponent as a free parameter**
- [ ] Know that **absolute and squared error are on different units** and can't be compared directly

## 📍 The warehouse location problem
*Notebooks: `Location Problem_Simple_guided.ipynb` · `Location Problem_Advanced_guided.ipynb`*

- [ ] Say why the problem is **unavoidably nonlinear** — distance is a **square root**
- [ ] Set up `Xcoord` and `Ycoord` as the decision variables, with store coordinates **fixed**
- [ ] Build the **`Dist` placeholder** — one slot per store — and pin it with one constraint each
- [ ] **Recognize this as the same trick as the regression problem** (that recognition is the point)
- [ ] Solve, plot the DC on the map, and sanity-check it against where you'd have guessed
- [ ] **Weight by deliveries:** multiply each distance by its store's trip count, **inside the constraint**
- [ ] Predict which way the DC moves when one store's trips are spiked, before you solve
- [ ] Explain what else you might weight by — revenue, urgency, fragility — and why the method doesn't care

## 🧠 The pattern worth memorizing

- [ ] State it cold: **a placeholder decision variable, one constraint per row pinning it to the model form, then summed in the objective**
- [ ] Show that the *same* pattern covers regression predictions **and** warehouse distances

---

## The one-sentence version

> **You can optimize problems that bend — fitting curves, placing warehouses — by parking a placeholder variable for every row and pinning it with a constraint; and you know that a nonlinear solver's "optimal" is only ever a local claim, so you check it from several starting points before you believe it.**
