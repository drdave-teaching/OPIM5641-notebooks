## The arc in one line

**Five stocks and a client who can't stomach a wild ride → covariance measures how stocks move together → risk is the *weighted* covariance matrix → solve it 100 times at 100 risk levels and read the trade-off off a plot.**

---

## Notebook 1 · `7_Nonlinear/Portfolio_Allocation_Womack.ipynb`

**Video 1 — Intro to Ms. Womack example** *(4:25)*
The setup, and Dave's honest reason for teaching it: *"exercises where you get to pretend you're a millionaire are the ones where students pay the most attention."* A client has picked five stocks across five industries — computer (~2% monthly), chemical (1%), power (0.69%), auto (2%), electronic (1.34%) — and two years of monthly returns behind them.

Then the two ideas that set up everything else. First, **the naive answer fails**: just buy the highest average return and your client rides *"up 20%, down 50%, up 70%, down 3%"* — **"that's too wild of a ride."** Second, and sharper, **"just diversify" isn't enough**: buy five stocks where two of them are NVIDIA and AMD and *"you might think you're diversifying, but if two of them do the same thing every day, you haven't done anything special."* Closes by plotting the raw series and anchoring it against the S&P's 7% return / 18% standard deviation.

## Notebook 2 · `7_Nonlinear/2_Covariance_and_Correlation.ipynb`

**Video 2 — Correlation and covariance** *(6:33)*
Opens with the constraint that shapes the whole model: **you cannot maximize and minimize simultaneously** — so instead of trading return against risk in one objective, we build **buckets of risk** and maximize return inside each.

Then the best analogy in the module. **Forget tech stocks — go to the Caribbean.** Rainy season, tourists buy umbrellas; sunny season, they buy ice cream. Each company alone makes money half the year and loses it the other half. *"What if instead you put half your money in umbrellas and half in ice cream? You'd achieve the same return with less volatility."* **That is modern portfolio theory in thirty seconds.**

The formula walk is careful: subtract each column's mean, multiply the paired deviations, sum over all 24 observations. Two things get landed hard — **why the diagonal is always positive** (`-3 × -3 = +9`; a stock always moves with itself), and **why magnitude matters** (computer at `0.009` versus boring old power at `0.001`).

Ends on the relationship — correlation is covariance over the product of the standard deviations, bounded to [-1, 1] — and then **answers the question students always ask**: *"we're only going to use the covariance in our model, and that's because we want the magnitude."*

## Notebook 3 · `7_Nonlinear/Portfolio_Allocation_Womack.ipynb`

**Video 3 — Calc_risk example** *(7:36)*
The build. Decision variables are the **proportion invested in each stock**; objective coefficients are the **average returns**. Then the trap: with no other constraints the solver dumps everything into auto — *"I like to think of that as Tesla. Set it and forget it. **But Ms. Womack is probably not going to like that.**"*

Constraints go on one at a time — proportions sum to one, beat a 1%-a-month savings account, risk greater than zero for technical reasons. Then the heart of it: **risk is the sum of the covariance matrix, weighted by the proportions you're investing.** Summing the raw matrix would give the same number every time; weighting each of the 25 cells by `prop_i × prop_j` is what makes it *dynamic* — and what makes it nonlinear.

Dave flags his own code honestly (*"this isn't the world's most elegant script… later on you'll make it more sophisticated"*) and warns where students trip: **the scale of the risk limits** differs wildly between this toy example and real market data.

*This video was trimmed from 8:17 — the original ended with a rushed even-split vs. concentrated comparison, which now lives in the notebook as **DEMO 1** and **DEMO 2**.*

**Video 4 — First outputs from Ms. Womack** *(5:11)*
Running it. The loop overwrites the risk constraint at each of 100 risk levels, solves, stores the allocation, and throws away the handful of infeasible solves that tiny numbers produce.

Then **the money plot**: at low risk you are **well diversified**, a little in each stock; as risk appetite grows you **dump everything into auto**. And the payoff plot — reward against risk — with the instruction that matters: **look for the top-left corner.** At a risk of 0.004 you get a 0.02 return; take on twice the risk and you gain only **10% more**. *"You don't have to double your risk to get twice the return."*

## The one thing to take away

**Return is linear. Risk is quadratic.** Return is a plain weighted average of the stocks you hold. Risk is built from the covariance matrix, because two stocks moving *together* is riskier than the same two moving independently. That single asymmetry is what makes this a nonlinear program — and it is the entire mathematical reason diversification works.

## Verified numbers

| Portfolio | Risk |
|---|---|
| Even split, 20% each | 0.001397 |
| 80% into Auto | 0.004813 |

The concentrated bet is **3.4× riskier** for barely more return.

## Gotcha

The returns CSV carries a **`Month` column with variance 50.0** — drop it before `.cov()` or it swamps the matrix. Both notebooks now drop it explicitly, with a comment saying why.
