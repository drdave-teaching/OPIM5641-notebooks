# M3.1 Video Guide — Linear Programming in Pyomo

**OPIM 5641 - Business Decision Modeling · Dr. Dave Wanik · University of Connecticut**

Modules 1 and 2 taught you to solve optimization problems **by hand** - enumerate them, draw them, pivot through them. Module 3 hands the whole thing to a **solver**. The point of doing it by hand first is that the solver is now a tool you understand rather than a magic spell.

## Notebook 1 · `6_Pyomo_LP/0_General_Framework_LP_Pyomo_blank.ipynb` + `_answers`

**Video 1 — Welcome to Module 3! Blank version of Pyomo guide** *(8:08)*
Why Pyomo, and **the five things you need to remember**: import it, get a blank `ConcreteModel()`, declare `Var`s (with `domain=NonNegativeReals` doing the real work), write an `Objective` with `sense=maximize`, then the constraints. Sets up the two-monitor workflow — blank on one screen, answers on the other. Includes the honest note on why the course left **Excel** behind: highlighting cells doesn't scale, `read_csv` does.

**Video 2 — My first Pyomo code!** *(10:04)*
The same five steps, now typed out on the Veerman furniture problem. Lands **\$8,400 at 0 chairs, 275 desks, 100 tables** — the same answer as brute force, graphical and Simplex — in **0.05 seconds**. Introduces `pprint()` as an audit tool, the "you declared **10 things**" framing, `SolverFactory` and CBC, and the parentheses trap: `model.profit` prints garbage, **`model.profit()`** prints the answer. Ends on the study rule: *don't use AI to write this code yet — internalize it first, then you can be a cognitive offloader.*

## Notebook 2 · `6_Pyomo_LP/1_Allocation_Models.ipynb`

**Video 3 — Intro to allocation models and shadow prices** *(5:32)*
The first of the three canonical shapes. **Allocation = hand out a limited resource among competing uses** → maximize, all $\le$ constraints. Then the pivot from *fitting* the model to **interrogating** it: fabrication is the **binding** constraint, and binding constraints are **the bottleneck jamming up your business**. Introduces the **shadow price** with the machine-cost framing (\$1/hour to run, \$3/hour of profit — any boss takes that deal), and the warning that it **decays with abundance**: *diamonds versus sand*.

**Video 4 — A transparent way to tackle shadow prices, binding constraints and sensitivity analysis** *(8:23)*
The method, and it's deliberately unglamorous: **put the model in a for loop** and vary the right-hand side of the binding constraint from 1,500 to 5,000 hours. No solver duals, nothing hidden. Read the product mix shifting (tables first, then desks, then desks cap out and **chairs appear**), then take `.diff()` on profit and normalize by the 100-hour step to get **\$3.99 per hour** — the shadow price, measured. Watch it decay to zero by ~3,000 hours, and land the business recommendation you'd actually take to a manager.

## Notebook 3 · `6_Pyomo_LP/2_CoveringModels.ipynb`

**Video 5 — Introduction to covering models** *(6:59)*
The second shape, and the mirror image of allocation: **minimize cost, all $\ge$ constraints** — meet every minimum standard as cheaply as possible. Delby Outfitters' trail mix. Notes which methods could solve it (brute force yes, **graphical no** — too many variables, Simplex yes but you'd need minimization). Solves for **\$7.53** — and then delivers the punchline: the optimal recipe is **half a pound of seeds, a third of raisins and 1.3 pounds of coconut flakes**. Technically perfect. Nobody would eat it.

**Video 6 — Enhancing the trail mix recipe** *(3:20)*
Put the business hat on. Add a floor of a little of everything, and cost rises to **\$8.33** for something you could actually sell. The modelling lesson underneath: those floors could equally have been **`bounds` on the variables** instead of five new constraints — *"I kind of like seeing the constraints, but it's your preference."* Closes with open-ended extensions (cap any one ingredient, force the bag to weigh exactly a pound).

## Notebook 4 · `6_Pyomo_LP/3_BlendingModels_Coffee.ipynb`

**Video 7 — Blending models (coffee and weighted averages)** *(8:28)*
The third shape. **Blending = hit a weighted-average spec**, and the wrinkle is that **solvers dislike fractions**, so the ratio has to be cleared by algebra before it can be a constraint. Builds the weighted average, sanity-checks it by setting $B=C=P=1$ (it collapses to the simple average), then multiplies through and collects terms to reach $-3B - 18C + 7P \ge 0$. Solves 4 million pounds for **\$2,448,000**, and then reads the *why* straight off the model: **aroma is binding**, Brazilian beans are cheap and aromatic so you use them all, Peruvian gets you to 78 but costs more, and Colombian is only sprinkled in for strength.
