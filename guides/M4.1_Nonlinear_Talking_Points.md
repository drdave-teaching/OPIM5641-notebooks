# M4.1 Talking Points — Nonlinear Optimization

**OPIM 5641 - Business Decision Modeling · Dr. Dave Wanik · University of Connecticut**

The ideas behind the nonlinear videos — what you should be able to explain without opening a notebook. Skills are what you can *do*; this is what you *understand*.

---

## 1 · Why the world stops being flat

**Not all problems are linear.** A power, a square root, an exponent, a stepwise function — **if any of those are part of your process, a linear solver is not going to work.** That's not a preference; the machinery genuinely doesn't apply.

**And sometimes nonlinear is the simpler description.** One curve can say what a dozen stitched-together linear pieces would need. Fewer pieces, less bookkeeping.

**But nonlinear costs you something**, and the rest of this module is about what.

**Three habits, stated up front:** *initialize* your decision variables and try several values; *bound* them when you can, because it helps convergence; and **if you can get away with a linear model, use it — they're more reliable.**

## 2 · The most important lesson in Module 4

**Two people run the same model and get different answers.** Initialize the iPhone pricing model at **0** and it reports *optimal*: 594 units at a price of 1.95. Initialize at **9** and it reports *optimal*: 900 units at 8.36. **Same code. Same data. Both say optimal.**

**The hill climber in the fog.** You're halfway up a mountain on a beautiful day. Thunderstorms roll in and the fog drops. You need the cabin at the top. You can't see, so you take a step and ask: *am I going up, or down?* And you keep stepping uphill until every direction goes down — **and then you announce you've reached the summit.**

**You have reached *a* summit.** That's a **local** optimum. In a linear program there was only ever one hill, so "no better neighbour" meant "best possible." **That guarantee is gone.**

**So plot the objective before you trust it.** The polynomial is visibly **bimodal** — two hills — and once you've seen the picture, the disagreement stops being a bug and becomes obvious.

**And defend your answer by starting from several places.** One run is an anecdote.

## 3 · Bounds help, and they also tell you things

Bounding keeps the search somewhere sensible and helps the solver converge. But watch what happens with bounds of $[1,7]$ starting at 5: the answer comes back **exactly 7** — sitting on the fence.

> **If your solution lands right on a boundary, pause and reevaluate.** It's the same instinct as a binding constraint in Module 3: the model is telling you the limit is what's driving the answer, not the shape of the problem.

## 4 · Regression is an optimization problem

**This is the bridge to every stats and ML class you've taken.** $y = a + bx$ — the only things you control are $a$ and $b$, so **those are your decision variables.** You want the pair that minimizes error.

**Error is a distance.** Squared error is a squared distance, which **emphasizes outliers** — a big miss hurts disproportionately. That's a *choice*, not a law.

**Get a reference answer first.** `scipy.linregress` takes one line and gives you the truth to check against: intercept about **\$4,435**, slope about **\$47** — meaning **each extra hour a pharmacy is open is worth about \$47 of revenue.** Interpretability is why people like linear models.

## 5 · The trick that makes it all work

**You make a placeholder decision variable** — `model.y` — with one slot per data point. Ten stores, ten slots, all empty and free to be anything.

**Then the constraints pin them down.** One per data point: *this prediction must equal $a + bx_i$.*

> **Up until now you haven't seen where the linear form of the model lives.** It isn't in the objective. **It's in the constraints.** That's the whole insight.

**Then the predictions flow into the objective**, one squared error at a time. *"You see all of these handoffs — it's almost like data management. But once you get your arms around it once, you have it forever."*

**And the proof it worked: Pyomo agrees with scipy exactly.**

## 6 · Two levers: the objective, and the form

**Change the objective.** Absolute error instead of squared error gives a **genuinely different line** — it stops chasing outliers. Neither is wrong; **you chose what counts as a big mistake.**

**But `abs()` breaks the solver** — the V has a point, and **functions with a point aren't easily differentiated.** The fix is a reformulation, and it works *because you're minimizing*.

**Change the form.** `x ** model.b` — with the exponent as a **free parameter**, so the solver finds the shape instead of you assuming it. Lower error, more believable curve.

**And know when to stop.** Seven hard-coded root terms will snake through every point. *"It's like going into Excel and cranking the polynomial order up, up, up."* **That's overfitting, and you'd never hand it to a boss.** The judgment is the job.

**One honest caution:** squared and absolute error are **on different units.** A smaller number doesn't automatically mean a better model.

## 7 · The warehouse problem is the same problem

**Distance is a square root**, so locating a facility is unavoidably nonlinear.

**And then the recognition that ties the module together:** you build a **`Dist` placeholder**, one slot per store, and **one constraint each** forcing it to equal the distance formula. *"Very sneaky — just like the linear form of the model."*

> **Regression:** *the prediction can be anything it wants, except it has to be the linear form of the model.*
> **Warehouse:** *the distance can be anything it wants, except it has to be Euclidean distance.*
>
> **Same trick. Different noun.**

## 8 · Weighting changes the question, not the method

The simple version treats every store as equal — that's a **centroid**. But trucks don't make equal trips. **Weight each distance by the store's number of deliveries, inside the constraint**, and each `Dist` becomes an *effective* distance while the objective stays a plain sum.

**Spike one store to 100 trips and the answer visibly drags toward it** — *"this is like Jupiter, right?"* — because the center of mass moved.

**You didn't change the algorithm. You changed what you were measuring.** And that generalizes: weight by revenue, urgency, fragility. **The method doesn't care what `n` means.**
